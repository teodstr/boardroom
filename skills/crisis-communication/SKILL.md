# Crisis communication (SCCT)

Protect people first, then the organization's reputation, with a response that matches how much responsibility the public attributes to the organization.

## When to use

- A crisis is happening or being simulated: accident, product harm or recall, misconduct, data breach, viral criticism.
- Preparing a crisis plan, or analyzing how a company handled a crisis.

## Inputs

1. What happened, when, where, and who is affected. What is confirmed and what is not.
2. The organization's history of similar crises and its reputation before the crisis.
3. The stakeholders involved and any legal or regulatory obligations (notification deadlines, recalls).

## Method

1. **Establish the facts.** Separate confirmed facts from unconfirmed reports. Never speculate in public.
2. **Protect people first.** The first messages give instructing information (what people should do to stay safe, for example stop using the product) and adjusting information (concern for those affected, what is being done). Reputation comes after.
3. **Identify the crisis type and its level of responsibility:**
   - **Victim cluster** (natural disaster, rumor, product tampering, workplace violence): weak attribution.
   - **Accidental cluster** (technical-error accident or product harm, challenges from stakeholders): minimal attribution.
   - **Preventable cluster** (human-error accident or product harm, organizational misdeed): strong attribution.
4. **Adjust for intensifiers.** A history of similar crises, or a poor reputation before the crisis, makes the public treat the crisis one level more severely.
5. **Choose the response strategy:**
   - **Deny** (attack the accuser, denial, scapegoating): only when the accusation is false (rumor, misinformation). Blaming others usually backfires.
   - **Diminish** (excuse, justification): for accidental crises without intensifiers.
   - **Rebuild** (compensation, apology): for preventable crises, and for accidental crises with intensifiers.
   - **Bolster** (reminding of past good works, praising stakeholders, or showing the organization is also a victim in the victim cluster): only as a complement to the other strategies.

   Never mix deny with diminish or rebuild: it destroys credibility.
6. **Map the stakeholders:** victims and customers, employees, authorities, media, investors, partners, online communities. For each one: what they need to know, the channel, the spokesperson, the timing.
7. **Draft the messages:**
   - A holding statement for the first hour: what we know, what we are doing, concern for those affected, when the next update will come.
   - Three key messages at most, repeated consistently.
   - Answers to the hardest questions.
8. **Plan the timeline** (first hour, first 24 hours, first week) and the monitoring (media coverage, online sentiment, call volumes).
9. **After the crisis:** draw the lessons and show proof of the corrective actions taken.

Ethical rule: never recommend lying, denying confirmed facts, or withholding information that people need for their safety or that the law requires. Recommend involving legal counsel before admitting liability.

## Quality bar

- People's safety comes before reputation in the first messages.
- The response strategy matches the crisis type and the intensifiers.
- Statements contain only confirmed facts, show empathy and announce the next update.
- Messages are consistent across stakeholders and channels.
- Nothing in the plan relies on deception.
- Written in the user's language, with the standard terms below.
